Challenge 03

How big is this, really?

3 illustrative possibilities

Read the challenge → Try your own idea →

A drop with a whole world inside

Illustrative example

First assumption: The large circle is an everyday object.

Thinking shift: Shrink the observer

Convincing clues: A glass slide, translucent organisms, internal structures and a microscope scale bar suggest microscopic pond life. This is an illustration, not a biological identification.

Another direction: Try a cross-section of a fruit rather than pond water.

An island park from above

Illustrative example

First assumption: The small circles are details on an object.

Thinking shift: Turn marks into a mapped landscape

Convincing clues: Trees, footbridges, tiny people, buildings and boats establish a large circular lake with islands.

Another direction: Make a crater research station or a flood-control basin.

A quiet shift above the clouds

Illustrative example

First assumption: The outer circle contains the three smaller objects.

Thinking shift: Separate frame scale from subject distance

Convincing clues: Bolted framing, handrails, control panels and a tethered mug make the outer circle a human-sized window; distant worlds lie beyond it.

Another direction: Use a diving-bell window with fish at different distances.

Challenge 04

What happened here?

3 illustrative possibilities

Read the challenge → Try your own idea →

The visitor that left a silver path

Illustrative example

First assumption: The broken line is a route drawn on a map.

Thinking shift: Read a line as a leftover trace

Convincing clues: A spiral shell, antennae, wet leaves and a silvery trail suggest a snail that has already crossed the paving.

Another direction: Try tyre marks, a dragged toy or ripples behind a duck.

A warm drink in a cooling room

Illustrative example

First assumption: The circle must have travelled along the line.

Thinking shift: Let the object stay and something escape

Convincing clues: A saucer, handle and coffee surface establish a stationary cup. Vapour fragments rise away from it, suggesting cooling over time.

Another direction: Try scent leaving a flower or sparks from a forge.

The wall just before it gives way

Illustrative example

First assumption: The marks describe something that already happened.

Thinking shift: Use motion to anticipate the next moment

Convincing clues: A crane cable, suspended ball, cracked brick wall and rubble turn the dashed arc into a motion cue and invite the next frame.

Another direction: Try a pendulum clock, playground swing or a ball approaching a window.

Challenge 05

What connects them?

3 illustrative possibilities

Read the challenge → Try your own idea →

Two rooftops, one shared garden

Illustrative example

First assumption: The squares are separate, self-contained objects.

Thinking shift: Connect places through shared use

Convincing clues: Building floors establish rooftops. A railed bridge, people, planters and tables turn them into a shared place.

Another direction: Make the squares two riverbanks linked by a ferry.

A conversation across two rooms

Illustrative example

First assumption: Similar squares must show the same thing.

Thinking shift: Make the pair complementary

Convincing clues: Two desks, headsets, screens with complementary puzzle pieces and two-way arrows suggest collaboration across rooms. The arrows are diagrammatic exchange cues.

Another direction: Try a sender and receiver, kitchen and dining room, or two musicians answering each other.

A window and a way of seeing

Illustrative example

First assumption: Both squares are objects of the same kind.

Thinking shift: Turn one into a representation of the other

Convincing clues: One frame is a divided window onto mountains. The other is an easel-mounted painting with related colours and simplified forms; brushes and a palette establish interpretation.

Another direction: Try a real garden beside its map, or a building beside its blueprint.

Challenge 07: Whose eyes are these?

A cat beneath the table

Lower the observer to cat height. Table legs hide part of the room, while the bird outside becomes the centre of attention.

Looking out from underwater

Put the observer underwater. Bubbles and fish establish the medium; the ladder suggests a route to the surface.

A small robot approaches a charging dock

Use a machine as the observer. A battery indicator and alignment guides make the same room a navigation task.

Challenge 08: What else could it do?

A tiny rain gauge

A hollow bowl can collect rain. The planted handle anchors it, while level marks turn a utensil into a measuring instrument.

A reflector for a lost traveller

A shiny surface can redirect light. Rays, a holding hand and a distant boat suggest signalling instead of eating.

A wind chime striker

A hard surface can make sound. Suspension, nearby tubes and motion marks suggest a wind chime striker.

Challenge 09: What’s missing?

Three empty places in a museum

Absence can tell a story. Empty labels and footprints suggest removal, but do not prove whether this was a theft, a loan or a new exhibition.

The tools have gone

A storage outline is evidence of an absent object. Pegs and an open toolbox suggest use or relocation without showing the missing tools.

Traces at a dig

Treat the outlines as traces in the ground. Survey markers and a brush invite reconstruction, while several explanations remain possible.

Challenge 10: Can both be true?

Welcome / Keep out

The unchanged shapes become a face, a door and a mat. Expression and access cues reverse the invitation.

Shelter / Obstruction

The wall can protect or obstruct. Heat and a resting figure make shade helpful; a destination beyond the wall makes it a barrier.

A welcome rain / A ruined picnic

The weather is identical, but the goal changes. Rain helps the garden and spoils an outdoor meal; both readings can be true.
